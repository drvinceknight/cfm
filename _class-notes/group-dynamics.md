## Timing

A full session. Roughly: 10 minutes on the categories and the framework,
25 minutes on the three scenarios, 10 minutes on effort, 10 minutes on
the chair's two practices, and the last 5 minutes on the closing slide.

Keep those last 5 minutes. Slide 11 is where this class's content
attaches to the form: the *disagreements and decisions* box is the thing
the whole session has been preparing them to fill in, and it is the
reminder to meet this week and bring the Week 5 minutes to the next
session.

## In class meeting

**The deck.** There is a write-on deck at
[/deck/group-dynamics/main.pdf]({{ site.baseurl }}/deck/group-dynamics/main.pdf).
The resolution for each of the four kinds of disagreement is blank on
slide 3, and each of the three scenarios on slides 5 to 7 has a
workspace and a short blank. Answers are at the end of these notes.

The opening claim is that disagreement is normal and often useful, and
that a group which never disagrees is either dominated by one person or
not engaging with the work. Say it plainly and early, because several
students will be carrying the opposite assumption.

Give the four categories (fact, approach, priority, effort) and the
four-step framework (listen, restate, identify, choose). Point out that
step 2, restating the other position, is the step that does most of the
work, and the one everybody skips.

Then the three scenarios. Put each on the screen, give the groups two or
three minutes to answer the questions among themselves, then take
answers from the room:

1. **Tests before or after the functions.** A disagreement of approach.
   The resolution is to try one and revisit, and to record the trial in
   the minutes. Watch for groups that want to resolve it by deciding who
   is more senior.
2. **The function runs, so it is correct.** A disagreement of fact,
   therefore the easiest kind: check the source, or better, write a test
   that distinguishes the two claims. Ask how long it should have taken.
   The answer is about two minutes, against the ten it did take.
3. **The function that is still not written.** A disagreement of effort.
   This is the one to spend time on. The private messaging is the part
   to pull out: it feels kind and it is the single most corrosive thing
   a group can do.

Handle effort directly. Raise it in a meeting, not outside one. State
observable facts rather than character judgements, and give them the two
phrasings side by side. Then connect it to the geometric mean: the
scheme is built so that neither free-riding nor carrying a group is
rewarded or punished unfairly, which is why the minutes matter.

Close on the chair's two weekly practices, round-robin discussion and
explicit decision checks. Acknowledge that both feel artificial at
first. Saying this out loud makes groups much more likely to persist
with them past the first meeting.

Then the closing slide, which does three jobs. It points at the
*disagreements and decisions* box, so today's framework has somewhere
concrete to land. It reminds them to rotate the chair and the secretary,
which is the week this starts to slip. And it reminds them to meet in
their own slot and bring the Week 5 minutes to the next session, the
second of the five \(I\) is counted from.

## Answers for the deck

**Slide 3, how would you settle each one?** The four blanks, in order:

- **Fact:** check the source. Look it up, or write a test that
  distinguishes the two claims.
- **Approach:** try one and revisit. Agree a review point and record the
  trial in the minutes.
- **Priority:** the chair decides. This is the role's one piece of real
  authority.
- **Effort:** raise it in a meeting, in terms of observable facts.

Take these from the room rather than reading them out. Fact and approach
come quickly; priority usually produces "vote", which is worth engaging
with, since a four-member group splitting two-two is exactly why the
chair exists.

**Slide 5, scenario 1.** A disagreement of **approach**: both want
working tested code and disagree on the route. The resolution is to try
one and revisit at the next meeting. Into the minutes goes the trial and
the review point, something like "agreed to write tests first for the
next function and review on Tuesday". Watch for groups that try to
resolve it by seniority or by who is the better coder.

**Slide 6, scenario 2.** A disagreement of **fact**, and therefore the
easiest kind. Right now they should write a test that distinguishes the
two claims, or check the function against a case they can compute by
hand. "It runs" and "it is correct" are different statements, which is
worth saying explicitly. It should have taken about two minutes, against
the ten it did take. The gap between those two numbers is the slide.

**Slide 7, scenario 3.** A disagreement of **effort**. The private
messaging is the part to pull out: it feels kind and considerate, and it
is the single most corrosive thing a group can do, because it moves the
problem somewhere D cannot answer it and the minutes cannot record it.
The right move is to raise it at the next meeting, in terms of the
observable fact that the function is not written, and to record what D
agrees to do before the following meeting. If it does not improve, it
comes to you in the contact hour.

**Slide 11, before you leave.** Nothing to elicit here, but do not let
it become a slide you talk over while people pack up. The point worth
making out loud is that the box exists because disagreement is expected:
a minutes form with an empty disagreements section every week for five
weeks is not evidence of a harmonious group, it is evidence that nobody
is recording what happened. Groups that write "none" every week are
worth a question in the contact hour.
