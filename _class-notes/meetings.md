## Timing

A full session. No meeting takes place in this hour: the groups hold
their own meeting in their timetabled group-work slot, and bring the
completed Week 5 minutes to the next class session.

| Section | Minutes |
|:--|--:|
| Framing: why the minutes matter, meeting against workshop | 4 |
| The four bad meetings (slides 4 to 7) | 20 |
| Roles and the standing agenda (slides 8 and 9) | 7 |
| Slide 10: the minutes, \(I\), and what happens next | 4 |
| Slides 11 to 15: this week's Python | 12 |
| Slides 16 to 19: what the project is, and the shape of a week | 5 |
| Slide 20: groups confirm their rota, slot and next chair | 8 |

If you are running late, cut a bad meeting rather than slide 20. A group
that leaves without a rota and an agreed time is the group whose next
meeting does not happen. The Python slides are the other thing not to
cut: a group that cannot write a working loop in Week 4 does not catch
up by Week 8 on its own.

Slides 16 to 19 are the orientation section: what the project actually
is, what a week looks like, the shape of the term, and what to do when a
group does not know what to do. They were added because several students
asked plainly what they were supposed to be doing. Go through them even
when the room looks confident, since the groups that need them are the
ones least likely to ask.

## In class meeting

**The deck.** There is a write-on deck at
[/deck/meetings/main.pdf]({{ site.baseurl }}/deck/meetings/main.pdf).
Each of the four bad meetings on slides 4 to 7 has a workspace under its
question, the three role cards on slide 8 are empty, and the rota, the
meeting slot and the next chair on slide 20 are blanks each group fills
in for itself. The four Python slides, 12 to 15, each have a workspace
for the answer the room gives. Answers are at the end of these notes.

The spine of this class is four meetings that went wrong. Each one is a
slide with a diagram and a question; put the question to the room and
let them answer before you give the reading.

Do **not** run a demonstration meeting, and no group meeting happens in
this hour. The bad examples do the work, and the groups hold their own
meeting in their timetabled slot afterwards.

Open with the meeting and workshop distinction. This is the single idea
that the rest of the class depends on: a meeting has an agenda and
produces decisions, a workshop has laptops and produces work. Most
student group sessions are a poor hybrid of the two.

Say what a workshop is, but do not tell them to hold one. The project
work is theirs to arrange, some of it alone and some of it together, and
how they divide it is not our business. What matters is that none of it
is the meeting. A group that works well on its own and meets for twenty
structured minutes a week is doing exactly the right thing.

Then the three purposes: inform, discuss, decide. The test to give them
is that if you cannot say which of the three an agenda item is, the item
is not ready.

Work through the four bad meetings:

1. **No agenda.** The timeline drifts from "where are we?" to a tangent
   about LaTeX fonts and ends with nothing decided. Ask what the
   secretary could possibly write. The answer, that there is nothing to
   write, is the point.
2. **A workshop in disguise.** Real work gets done, a genuine bug is
   fixed, and the meeting fails anyway: three agenda items are never
   reached and the quiet member contributed nothing and is recorded as
   having done nothing. Ask what it costs D specifically. This connects
   back to \(I\).
3. **One person ran it.** A talks for 35 of 40 minutes, everyone agrees,
   the minutes look immaculate. Ask what is missing. Steer towards the
   observation that unanimous agreement with no discussion is evidence
   of a problem, not of a healthy group.
4. **No record.** Nothing goes wrong in the room at all. A week later
   nobody can remember who agreed to what. Ask what it costs the group,
   then what it costs each member.

Draw the four together: the common failures are no agenda, no separation
of meeting from workshop, no chair making space, and no secretary. The
three roles fix all four, and they rotate weekly.

Finish the taught part with the standing agenda, then slide 10, which is
the one that matters: the minutes are where \(I\) comes from, the group
meets in its own slot this week, and the completed form comes to the
next session.

## This week's Python

Slides 11 to 15 cover the week's two chapters, variables with
conditionals and loops, and functions with data structures. They are not
a lecture on either: the students read the chapters themselves, and
these slides put the four things that actually catch people in front of
the room. Each one is a question with a workspace under it, so let the
room answer before you do.

**Slide 12, one equals sign or two?** The code assigns inside a
condition, which is a syntax error in Python rather than a silent bug.
One sign assigns a value, two signs ask a question. Worth saying that
other languages let this through silently and Python does not, because
the error message is the thing they will actually meet.

**Slide 13, the loop that never stops.** Nothing in the body changes
`number`, so the condition stays true forever. The missing line is
`number = number + 1`. The general rule is worth more than the fix: read
the loop out loud and ask what changes the condition. If nothing does,
it never stops.

**Slide 14, when is something a function?** The prompt is that they have
written the same three lines twice. The answer is that it wants a name,
and that giving it one is what a function is for. Point at the docstring
while you are there: it is part of the Documentation mark in Week 9, not
decoration.

**Slide 15, list, tuple, set or dictionary?** Four examples, four cards,
and the room fills them in. The roots of a polynomial are a list, a
point in the plane is a tuple, the vertices of a graph are a set, and a
lookup from name to mark is a dictionary. The reasoning matters more
than the answers: does order matter, can there be repeats, does it
change after you build it. Push every answer back to the mathematics
rather than to Python.

## Closing

Then slide 20, and give the groups the last eight minutes on it in the
room. They need three things settled before they leave: the secretary
rota for the five meeting weeks, when and where they will meet, and who
chairs the next one. Walk the room while they do it. A group that cannot name a
time and a chair will not meet this week, and that is the failure you
can still prevent from here.

Two things to flag while you are at it, since they shape the first form.
A group that writes only a list of names has recorded attendance, not
engagement, and the form asks for what each member contributed. A group
of three present and one absent should still record the absent member
and say so; a member who is genuinely unwell and catches up through the
minutes can still be recorded as engaged in a later week.

## Answers for the deck

**Slide 2, which one have your group sessions been so far?** Almost always
the honest answer is neither cleanly: an unstructured hybrid. That is the
point of asking, and it lands better coming from them than from you.

**Slide 4, what does the secretary write?** There is nothing to write.
No decision was taken, no action was assigned, and no information was
shared that anybody could act on. Push until someone says this out loud,
because it is the whole argument for an agenda: the agenda is what makes
it possible to tell whether the meeting succeeded.

**Slide 5, what did it cost D?** The group got a good bug fix, so the
cost is invisible at group level. D contributed nothing, so the minutes
record nothing against D's name, so D's \(I\) takes the hit for a session
in which the group did real work. Expect someone to object that this is
unfair. It is the right objection: the fix is not to mark D differently,
it is for the chair to have stopped the meeting becoming a workshop.

**Slide 6, what is missing?** Disagreement. Three decisions were taken
and nobody questioned any of them, which means either the decisions were
not really examined or the other three did not feel able to speak.
Unanimity with no discussion is evidence of a problem, not of health.
The chair prevents it with round-robin discussion and by checking each
decision member by member, which is next week's material.

**Slide 7, what did it cost?** Two things, and students usually only get
the first. The group loses the decisions and has to retake them. Each
member individually loses the evidence of their engagement, because the
minutes are what \(I\) is computed from. A meeting that is not recorded
did not happen as far as the mark is concerned.

**Slide 8, what do the three roles do, and how often do they change?**

- **Chair:** writes and circulates the agenda beforehand, keeps to time,
  brings every member in, and calls the decision when the group is split.
- **Secretary:** writes the minutes during the meeting: who attended,
  what was discussed, what was decided, what actions were assigned.
- **Members:** everyone else, and the role is active. Contribute, raise
  questions, accept actions.

They **rotate every week**, so every member does each role over the five
meeting weeks. Groups reliably guess chair and secretary and then say
members "just attend"; that is the one to correct.

**Slide 20, the four items.** The first three are filled in by each
group for itself, not by you, and they are the ten minutes of group work
in this session. A group that cannot name next week's chair before
leaving is the group to check on first. The fourth is the one to say out
loud rather than leave them to read: they meet in their own slot this
week, and the completed Week 5 minutes come to the next class session.
