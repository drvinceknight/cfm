In class meeting

The spine of this class is four meetings that went wrong. Each one is a slide with a diagram and a question; put the question to the room and let them answer before you give the reading.

Do not run a demonstration meeting, and no group meeting happens in this hour. The bad examples do the work, and the groups hold their own first meeting in their timetabled slot afterwards.

Open with the meeting and workshop distinction. This is the single idea that the rest of the class depends on: a meeting has an agenda and produces decisions, a workshop has laptops and produces work. Most student group sessions are a poor hybrid of the two.

Then the three purposes: inform, discuss, decide. The test to give them is that if you cannot say which of the three an agenda item is, the item is not ready.

Work through the four bad meetings:

  1. No agenda. The timeline drifts from "where are we?" to a tangent about LaTeX fonts and ends with nothing decided. Ask what the secretary could possibly write. The answer, that there is nothing to write, is the point.
  2. A workshop in disguise. Real work gets done, a genuine bug is fixed, and the meeting fails anyway: three agenda items are never reached and the quiet member contributed nothing and is recorded as having done nothing. Ask what it costs D specifically. This connects back to \(I\).
  3. One person ran it. A talks for 35 of 40 minutes, everyone agrees, the minutes look immaculate. Ask what is missing. Steer towards the observation that unanimous agreement with no discussion is evidence of a problem, not of a healthy group.
  4. No record. Nothing goes wrong in the room at all. A week later nobody can remember who agreed to what. Ask what it costs the group, then what it costs each member.

Draw the four together: the common failures are no agenda, no separation of meeting from workshop, no chair making space, and no secretary. The three roles fix all four, and they rotate weekly.

Finish the taught part with the standing agenda, then slide 10, which is the one that matters: the minutes are where \(I\) comes from, the group meets in its own slot this week, and the completed form comes to the next session.

Then slide 11, and give the groups ten minutes on it in the room. They need three things settled before they leave: the secretary rota for the five meeting weeks, when and where they will meet, and who chairs the first one. Walk the room while they do it. A group that cannot name a time and a chair will not meet this week, and that is the failure you can still prevent from here.

Two things to flag while you are at it, since they shape the first form. A group that writes only a list of names has recorded attendance, not engagement, and the form asks for what each member contributed. A group of three present and one absent should still record the absent member and say so; a member who is genuinely unwell and catches up through the minutes can still be recorded as engaged in a later week.

Answers for the deck

Slide 2, which one have your group sessions been so far? Almost always the honest answer is neither cleanly: an unstructured hybrid. That is the point of asking, and it lands better coming from them than from you.

Slide 4, what does the secretary write? There is nothing to write. No decision was taken, no action was assigned, and no information was shared that anybody could act on. Push until someone says this out loud, because it is the whole argument for an agenda: the agenda is what makes it possible to tell whether the meeting succeeded.

Slide 5, what did it cost D? The group got a good bug fix, so the cost is invisible at group level. D contributed nothing, so the minutes record nothing against D's name, so D's \(I\) takes the hit for a session in which the group did real work. Expect someone to object that this is unfair. It is the right objection: the fix is not to mark D differently, it is for the chair to have stopped the meeting becoming a workshop.

Slide 6, what is missing? Disagreement. Three decisions were taken and nobody questioned any of them, which means either the decisions were not really examined or the other three did not feel able to speak. Unanimity with no discussion is evidence of a problem, not of health. The chair prevents it with round-robin discussion and by checking each decision member by member, which is next week's material.

Slide 7, what did it cost? Two things, and students usually only get the first. The group loses the decisions and has to retake them. Each member individually loses the evidence of their engagement, because the minutes are what \(I\) is computed from. A meeting that is not recorded did not happen as far as the mark is concerned.

Slide 8, what do the three roles do, and how often do they change?

  • Chair: writes and circulates the agenda beforehand, keeps to time, brings every member in, and calls the decision when the group is split.
  • Secretary: writes the minutes during the meeting: who attended, what was discussed, what was decided, what actions were assigned.
  • Members: everyone else, and the role is active. Contribute, raise questions, accept actions.

They rotate every week, so every member does each role over the five meeting weeks. Groups reliably guess chair and secretary and then say members "just attend"; that is the one to correct.

Slide 11, the four items. The first three are filled in by each group for itself, not by you, and they are the ten minutes of group work in this session. A group that cannot name next week's chair before leaving is the group to check on first. The fourth is the one to say out loud rather than leave them to read: they meet in their own slot this week, and the completed Week 4 minutes come to the next class session.
